Computational Approaches to the Non-Observed EconomyPolicy and practice

Module 8Designing counter-NOE policy

Policy against the hidden economy serves four different goals, and one instrument can help one goal while hurting another. With an invented ten-year model of audits, digital reporting and services, run through a thousand possible futures, we turn policy into a decision problem, keep six sources of uncertainty apart, compare criteria (average, bad tail, worst case, chance of reaching a target), see why learned audit rules need a little randomness and why adaptive rules need timely numbers, and then read a Pareto front, choose by weights, limits or priorities, find the assumptions behind the spread, and ask whether the recommendation changes.

By the end you should be able to write a policy as a decision problem, compare criteria under uncertainty, read and choose on a Pareto front, run global and decision sensitivity with the value of information, report true, observed and unseen activity separately, and plan a pilot and an adaptive rollout.

Four packages, a thousand futures. Four policy packages for our invented Mainland, each costing about the same. We ran each one through a thousand possible futures. On average, the package that funds services and formalisation wins. After ten years, it leaves about 15 percent of the economy hidden. But in the worst tenth of futures, a different package wins: the one that invests in digital reporting. Best is always best by some measure, under some assumptions. This module is about choosing policy when the truth is uncertain and every choice has a cost.

Four steps. Welcome to Module 8: designing policy against the non-observed economy. I'm Teddy. In this video: what counts as policy here, how to turn it into a decision problem, the kinds of uncertainty, the ways to optimise under them, and how to fit the models that predict a policy's effect.

Goals and instruments. Policy against the hidden economy is much more than audits and fines. It includes third-party reporting, e-invoicing, traceable payments, easier registration, simpler rules, social protection, public services and trust. And it can aim at four different things. Reducing the true amount of hidden activity. Formalising workers and firms. Making activity visible to the authorities. Or reducing harm, like protecting workers, even before full formalisation is possible. One instrument can help one goal and hurt another. Digital reporting can make activity visible without reducing it. An aggressive audit campaign can raise revenue while pushing vulnerable workers away and eroding trust. So we describe the state with several numbers: the true hidden share, how much the authority can observe, trust, and the wider context. And the policy with several instruments: audits, digital reporting, and services. A policy can be fixed, a schedule over years, or a rule that reacts to new data. And it has to respect budgets, capacity, law, fairness and risk.

2:00

Whom to audit. Take a classic decision: whom to audit. On paper, it's simple. Pick the firms with the highest expected recovery, until the budget runs out. But every number in that formula is uncertain. And one of them hides a trap: the risk estimates are learned from past audits, and past audits weren't random. We invented 2,000 firms in four sectors. One sector is the riskiest: more than half of its firms evade. But an old rule never audited it. A model trained on past audits finds an evader in 55 percent of its next 200 audits. That beats random audits, at 37. But only 27 percent of its picks come from the riskiest sector. It can't learn about firms it has never seen. Add six percent of audits chosen at random, just for learning. The next model finds an evader 64 percent of the time, almost as well as if we knew the true risks. And 85 percent of its picks now come from the riskiest sector. A little randomness buys a lot of knowledge.

3:02

Average, bad tail, worst case. Uncertainty comes from six places. The measurement itself. Unknown response coefficients. Which mechanism is right. How people adapt. Whether the policy is delivered as planned. And shocks from outside. Keep them apart. If the measured hidden share rises after a reform, it could mean more hiding, better detection, or a new method of measurement. Each calls for a different response. Our Mainland model has three instruments and three states: the hidden share, observability, and trust. Every response coefficient is uncertain, so each of the thousand futures draws its own, plus recessions and noise. The simplest criterion is the average outcome. Services first wins, at about 15 percent. Enforcement first gets 20, digital reporting 22, and the status quo 25. But averages hide bad tails. Look at the worst ten percent of futures. There, digital reporting does best, at 27 percent. Services first reaches 28, because it depends on an effect we're unsure about. Enforcement first reaches 33. In its single worst future, enforcement first ends at 48 percent. When trust collapses, heavy audits backfire. One caution about scenarios. Running more random seeds doesn't fix an equation that's wrong. The scenarios have to span the uncertainty that matters, including the mechanisms themselves. So report more than one number for every package: its average, its bad tail, and its worst case. A ranking that holds under all three is worth more than a win on any single one.

4:29

Robust choices and chance targets. That's the idea of robust optimisation: choose the policy whose worst plausible outcome is acceptable. It needs no probabilities, only a set of plausible values. Its danger is being too cautious, if that set includes combinations that can't happen together. A middle way protects against a range of possible distributions, not just one. That matters here, because a policy can change the very patterns it was trained on. Or set a chance constraint: the hidden share must end below 22 percent with high probability. Services first gets there in 72 percent of futures, enforcement in 59, digital reporting in 47, the status quo in 23. How high is high enough? Ninety percent? Ninety-five? That number is a policy choice, not a statistical constant.

5:14

Adaptive policy and late information. A fixed plan isn't always safest. A government can pilot, watch, and adjust. For example, raise audits when the hidden share is above target, and lower them when it's below. But the hidden share arrives late. We ran that rule three ways: with last year's number, with a one-year delay, and with a three-year delay. With fresh numbers, it overshot the target by about five points and used twelve audit-years. With a three-year delay, it overshot by eight, and used fifteen. It kept pushing long after the job was done. When the model is a simulation, the objective is a noisy black box. Start with a plain grid or random search, then re-test the finalists in many fresh futures. A clever optimiser can fail silently. And remember the practical limits. Instruments have start-up costs, delays and saturation. Assigning money to e-invoicing doesn't mean everyone adopts it, and assigning an audit doesn't mean it gets done. These limits belong inside the model, not in a footnote. A plan that ignores them will look better on paper than it does in the field.

6:18

Fitting the response model. All of this rests on a model of how the world responds to policy. Fitting the past is one question. Whether the policy's effect is identified, for the range you plan to use, is another. Here's a trap. In our invented history, audits rose in year six. In the same year, the statistics office changed its method, which made the measured hidden share three points lower. Calibrate without knowing that, and the model credits audits with the drop. It estimates an audit effect of 0.12, more than twice the true 0.05. Add a term for the method change, and the estimate falls to 0.08. Closer, but still off, because both changes happened in the same year. The data can't fully separate them. When a likelihood is out of reach, simulated moments, approximate Bayesian computation or synthetic likelihood can still fit a simulator. And always ask who could enter the data, and why an outcome was observed. Evaluate the model where decisions are made: near budget limits and near the points where the preferred policy changes. And treat published effects from other countries as ranges, not as universal constants.

7:25

Recap. Let's recap. Counter-NOE policy has four distinct goals, and instruments can help one while hurting another. Learned audit rules inherit past selection. A little random auditing fixes a lot. The best policy depends on the criterion: average, bad tail, worst case, or a chance target. Adaptive rules need timely information, and fitted effects need identification. In the lab, you'll choose a package for the Mainland and see how its ranking changes with the criterion. Only 21 are not beaten on both cost and hidden share at once. Every other mix wastes money or leaves hiding on the table. Choosing among those 21 is not a calculation. It's a decision. 0:39

Many objectives. A government cares about many things at once: hidden activity, revenue, worker protection, jobs, the burden on small firms, fair auditing, privacy, trust, and regional equity. One policy dominates another if it's no worse on every objective and better on at least one. The undominated policies form the Pareto front. The front removes waste. It doesn't pick a winner. On our front, spending moves the hidden share from 32 percent with no policy at all down to 12 at the full budget. The early steps buy the most. A weighted sum is the easy way to pick one. But watch the weights. Put a fifth of the weight on cost, and you choose the full-budget mix. Half the weight, and you choose light audits only. Most of the weight on cost, and you choose to do nothing. Small changes in weights, completely different policies. And weighted sums can miss parts of a front that isn't convex. So show the weights you used, and check how far they can move before the choice changes. If a small shift flips the answer, say so. A limit is often clearer. Minimise hidden activity, subject to trust staying at least one half and the cost at most 0.8. That picks moderate audits plus services, ending near 16 percent. Strict priorities, targets with allowed deviations, and evolutionary search for messy simulated fronts are other options. Choose the method that matches how the objectives are actually stated.

1:58

Constraints, horizon, combinations. Set the objectives before running anything, and don't choose weights after seeing which policy they favour. Fairness can be a constraint, like a cap on the difference in audit rates between regions. But equal rates aren't automatically fair if risk differs. The definition has to follow the purpose and the law. Some things shouldn't be traded at all. If no revenue gain can justify a privacy violation, privacy is a constraint, not an objective. The same goes for due process and minimum services. The horizon matters too. Some instruments pay back quickly, others need years of investment first. State the horizon and the discounting, and check that the choice survives other reasonable ones. And look for combinations. Digital records make audits sharper. Services make digital adoption easier. Some pairs work better together, and some work against each other. When you present results, show a few named packages, not hundreds of points. For each one: the average, the bad tail, the cost, who bears it, and the assumptions under which it stops being the best choice.

3:00

Sensitivity analysis. Next, sensitivity analysis. Uncertainty analysis describes the spread of outcomes. Sensitivity analysis asks which assumptions create it. Local derivatives are quick but see only a neighbourhood. Changing one input at a time is easy to explain, but misses interactions. Screening methods cheaply find the inputs worth a closer look. Global methods vary everything at once over its plausible range, and split the output's variance into contributions. The first-order index counts an input alone. The total index adds all its interactions. For the robust policy, the services effect explains the most, with a total index of 0.45. The audit effect follows, at 0.35, then the baseline drift, at 0.13. The first-order indices add up to only about 0.8. The rest comes from interactions, which a one-at-a-time analysis would never see. Use good sampling, check that the indices have converged, and don't choose the ranges after seeing the results. A ranking of inputs is not a ranking of causes. Beyond parameters, change the model itself: different decision rules for agents, different peer networks, a different benchmark. Some assumptions can't be tested by sweeping a number. And remember equifinality from Module 7. Test every candidate policy across all the calibrations that fit the data, not just the one that fits best.

4:25

Decision sensitivity and the value of information. But the question that matters for policy is simpler. Does the recommended policy change? We varied the services effect alone. If it's essentially zero, enforcement first wins. At small values, the robust mix wins, then the mix that's best on average. From a modest value upward, services first wins. So the decision hinges on one number we don't know well. That tells you where to spend research money. If we could learn the services effect before choosing, the expected hidden share would fall by about 1.8 points. Learning the audit effect is worth 0.7. Learning the direct effect of digital reporting, almost nothing. Run the pilot that could change your mind. And turn the influential quantities into monitoring indicators, with stopping rules written down in advance. A sensitivity analysis that ends with a table of indices is only half done. The useful ending is a sentence a decision maker can act on: which number to learn, how to learn it, and what you'd do with each answer.

5:25

The study, end to end. Now the whole study, end to end. The best mix on average spends on light audits and lots of services, and ends near 12 percent. Its worst tenth reaches 23. The robust mix adds a little digital reporting and more audits. Its average is a bit worse, near 13.5. Its worst tenth is better, at 22. And the knee of the front, where extra money starts buying much less, is audits alone at 40 percent of the budget, ending near 21 percent. One more lesson from the digital package. As observability rises, the part of hiding that the authority can see grows from 12 points to 18, while true hiding falls from 27 to 22. Read the rising detections as rising hiding, and you'd cancel a policy that's working. Report true, observed and unseen separately. All of these numbers come from an invented teaching model, not from real policy effects. Their value is in the workflow they demonstrate.

6:21

From model to study. Finally, turn the model into a study. First, pilot in a few regions, varying the instruments that drive the uncertainty, and measure adoption, appeals, survival, jobs, trust and payment channels. Second, recalibrate an ensemble of models with the pilot data, and prefer policies that stay near the front under all of them. A policy that's optimal only under one fragile equation shouldn't be scaled up. Third, implement adaptively, with the rules, indicators and review dates announced in advance. Adapting doesn't mean changing the objective after seeing the results. And communicate honestly. One package minimises the average, another the bad tail, a third balances cost, trust and hiding. Show the ranges and the assumptions. The choice belongs to the public. Each step leaves a record of what was assumed, what was tested and what was decided. That record lets someone else check the study, or redo it when new data arrive.

7:17

Recap. Let's recap. The Pareto front removes waste. Weights, limits and priorities choose, and the method should match how the goals are stated. Global sensitivity finds the assumptions behind the spread. Decision sensitivity asks whether the recommendation changes, and the value of information says what to learn next. Report true, observed and unseen activity separately, and move from model to pilot to adaptive policy. In the lab, you'll explore the front, change the weights and limits, and run a sensitivity analysis on your favourite package.
