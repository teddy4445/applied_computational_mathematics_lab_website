Symbolic Physical Laws DiscoveryTrust and beyond

Module 9Trustworthy equation discovery

A found law is a claim, and a claim needs its uncertainty, its tests and its record. With our own simulations of a tank draining through a small hole (Torricelli's law) we separate four sources of uncertainty; check whether error bars are calibrated by repeating the experiment in simulation; meet parameters the data can never separate and a short experiment where 79 different formulas fit; and count one law written 61 ways as one. Then we test like we mean it: 21 pipelines, splits that match the claim, a polynomial that summarises but can't predict, a bigger hole and a syrup, strong baselines, negative and positive controls and ablations. Finally we design the experiment that falsifies rival laws, close the loop, put physical sense and stability on the Pareto front, record what reproducibility needs, and keep people in the loop.

By the end you should be able to name and check the four sources of uncertainty, test calibration, recognise structural and practical non-identifiability and equivalent formulas, run many pipelines and splits that match the claim, use shift tests, baselines, controls and ablations, design discriminating experiments, and report results that others can reproduce.

0:00

Error bars that aren't. A tank of water drains through a small hole, and a sensor reads the level every two seconds, to the millimetre. A fit gives the law: the level falls at a rate proportional to the level to a power. The fitted power is 0.5005, give or take 0.0006. But how sure is that? We repeated the experiment two hundred times in simulation, with fresh noise each time, and checked how often the ninety-percent error bars of three common pipelines contained the true power, one half. Finite differences with a log-log fit: twelve percent of the time. Smoothing first: twenty percent. Fitting whole trajectories: ninety percent, as promised. Same data, same tank. Only the pipeline differed. An error bar is a claim too, and it can be wrong. This video is about how sure we can be. This is Module nine: trustworthy equation discovery. Part one: the sources of uncertainty. Part two: calibrating error bars. Part three: identifiability, when the data can't decide. Part four: when different formulas mean the same thing.

1:09

A draining tank. Our tank is a cylinder with a cross-section of five hundred square centimetres, draining through a one-centimetre hole. Torricelli worked out the law in the sixteen-forties: water leaves the hole as fast as if it had fallen from the surface, so the level drops at a rate proportional to the square root of the level. From a full metre, our tank empties in about eight minutes. We simulated three drains, from one metre, seventy centimetres and forty centimetres, with one millimetre of sensor noise. The sensor stops below two centimetres, so a drain from one metre gives two hundred and six readings, one every two seconds. We wrote the law as: the rate of fall equals a constant c times the level to a power p, and let the fit find both. The truth is p equals one half. The constant c depends on the hole and on the tank. We'll come back to it in part three. One consequence you can see by eye: the tank slows down as it empties. From a full metre, the level falls to half a metre in only about thirty percent of the drain time. The last half metre takes the other seventy.

2:13

Sources of uncertainty. Part one: where uncertainty comes from. First, noise: the sensor's millimetre. Second, the parameters: the same data allow a range of constants. Third, the structure: other formulas may fit as well. And fourth, the pipeline: how we estimate derivatives, which library, which threshold. Each needs its own check. Noise and parameters give error bars. Structure needs competing laws. And the pipeline needs the same analysis run several ways, which is the next video's subject. Many reports give only the second. A trustworthy one names all four, and says which were checked. For our tank: the noise is one millimetre. The error on the power, fitted on three drains, is 0.0006. One rival law, a rate that is a straight line in the level, fits the first minute and a half as well as Torricelli's law. And changing the pipeline alone moved the power by three hundredths. A single number with a single error bar covers only the first two.

3:10

Calibrating error bars. Part two: calibration. A ninety-percent interval should contain the truth in nine experiments out of ten. That's checkable, if you can repeat the experiment, or simulate it. The finite-difference pipeline takes differences of neighbouring readings, then fits a straight line on a log-log plot. Its power came out at 0.53, biased, and its intervals contained one half in only twelve percent of the experiments. Smoothing first removed the bias, but the intervals were far too narrow, because the fit treats smoothed derivatives as independent measurements, which they're not. Twenty percent. Why does independence matter so much? Smoothing makes neighbouring derivatives share most of their noise. A fit that believes it has two hundred independent readings thinks it knows more than it does, and its interval shrinks to match. Fitting the solution of the law directly to the readings, where the noise really is independent, gave intervals that covered the truth in ninety percent of the experiments, and they were the narrowest of the three. The trajectory intervals were 1.8 thousandths wide. The smoothing ones were wider, 3.1 thousandths, and still too narrow: their estimates scattered over 16 thousandths, on both sides of one half. If you can't repeat the real experiment, simulate it: use your fitted law and your noise level to make many synthetic copies, refit each one, and count how often the intervals cover the law you simulated. Check your error bars the way you'd check a law: on data where you know the answer.

4:40

Identifiability. Part three: identifiability. The law's constant depends on three physical quantities: the discharge coefficient of the hole, the hole's area, and the tank's area. Only their combination reaches the data. Take three holes. The first has a coefficient of 0.6. The second has 0.8, and a slightly smaller diameter. The third has 0.48, and a larger one. The three tanks drain identically, to the last digit. No amount of level data can separate them. You'd need another measurement, like the size of the hole. The fix isn't a better fit. Either add a measurement that separates them, or report only the combination the data can see: here, the constant c, 0.0042. This is common in real systems. A rate constant and a concentration that only ever appear as a product, or a mass and a stiffness seen only through their ratio: the data see the combination, never the parts. That's structural non-identifiability. There's a practical kind too. Watch the tank for only forty seconds, a drop of sixteen centimetres, and the power could be anything from 0.19 to 0.84: from about a fifth to over four fifths. In that short window, the constant and the power trade against each other: a bigger power with a bigger constant draws almost the same curve. Their estimates were correlated at 0.96. Watch the whole drain, and the range shrinks to 0.499 to 0.501. Practical non-identifiability can be fixed with data: a longer drain, or drains from several levels, each pinning down the trade-off from a different side. When parameters trade off, the data haven't decided. Report the trade-off, or design data that break it.

6:20

When formulas mean the same thing. Part four: when different formulas mean the same thing. We asked a small search to try every formula of up to five symbols, built from the level, one, two, square roots, logs, exponentials and the four operations, each with one fitted constant: 1,629 formulas. Sixty-one of them fit the full drain as well as Torricelli's law, within two percent. All sixty-one are Torricelli's law: the square root of h, h over the square root of h, the square root of h plus h, the square root of the log of e to the h. One law, written sixty-one ways. Counting them as sixty-one discoveries would be wrong. Simplify, or compare the formulas numerically at many points, before you count. A practical test: after fitting their constants, evaluate two formulas at a few hundred random points, inside and outside the data range. If they agree everywhere to many digits, they are one law. A computer algebra system can often confirm it symbolically. The best formula that was really different, h plus the fourth root of h, fit 4.2 times worse than the truth. On the full drain, the data chose clearly. That's the pattern to look for. A wide gap between the best fit and the next different formula means the data have chosen. A crowd of near-ties means they haven't. Now the short, forty-second drain. Five hundred and ninety-two formulas fit it as well as the truth, and they are seventy-nine different functions. Near the bottom of the tank, at three centimetres, they disagree wildly: some even predict the level rising. Remember that the next time a search returns a surprising formula from a short experiment. Many formulas fitting the same data isn't a surprise. It's a measurement of how little the data say.

7:57

Recap. Let's recap. Uncertainty has four sources: noise, parameters, structure and pipeline. Each needs its own check. Error bars must be calibrated: on repeated or simulated experiments, a ninety-percent interval should cover the truth nine times in ten. Some combinations of parameters can never be separated by the data, and some only by better data. And formulas must be compared as functions, not as strings. Twenty-one pipelines, one tank. We took one tank and one kind of data, and analysed them twenty-one ways: three ways to estimate derivatives, two ways to fit, and three slices of the data, plus fitting whole trajectories. The lowest power was 0.488. The highest was 0.531. All twenty-one were within 0.05 of one half; nineteen within 0.02. The two outliers share one choice: differences of noisy neighbours, fitted on a log-log plot. Each pipeline ran on twenty noise draws, on each of the three slices; the numbers here are the medians. Twenty-one numbers, one tank. A result that survives many reasonable pipelines is a robust result. This video is about testing a law as if you meant it. This is Module nine, video two. 1:07

Robustness across pipelines. Part one. A pipeline is every choice between the raw readings and the reported law: derivatives, smoothing, library, threshold, fitting method, and which data were kept. Each choice is reasonable; none is the only option. So run several. If the law appears in most reasonable pipelines, report that, with the spread. If it appears in only one, then that choice is the result, not the tank. A practical way to do this: write the pipeline once, with each choice as a setting, then run every combination you would be willing to defend. The table of results is the robustness check. Running many pipelines isn't hiding behind an average. If they disagree, the disagreement is itself a finding: the data alone don't settle the question. In our table, nineteen pipelines agreed to within 0.02, and that agreement is what we can stand behind. Here, the spread itself was informative. Both outliers took logs of noisy differences of neighbouring readings, which distorts small rates the most, near the bottom of the tank. That's the most likely source of their bias. The slice of data mattered too. On the top half of the tank alone, the pipelines leaned low: the lowest was 0.488. On the bottom half, they leaned high: the highest was 0.525. Trajectory fitting gave 0.500 on all three slices. Robustness is a distribution over pipelines, not a single run.

2:26

Splits that match the claim. Part two: the split must match the claim. Our claim: this law describes the tank from any starting level, and for any small hole. So we compared it with a flexible rival: a degree-four polynomial in the level. Matching the split to the claim isn't extra rigour. It's what the claim means: any starting level means testing a level you didn't train on. Hold out random readings, and both do fine: one millimetre for the law, 1.3 for the polynomial. Hold out a whole drain from a new starting level inside the range: 1.2 and 1.4. Now a split that matches the claim. Train above forty centimetres, and predict a drain from thirty. The law: 1.3 millimetres. The polynomial: fifty-nine, and three times out of twenty its forecast blew up. Why? Inside the range, five numbers can bend a polynomial along any smooth curve. Outside it, nothing ties the polynomial to the physics: even at an empty tank, its rate isn't zero. Torricelli's law has that limit built in. At zero level, the square root is zero, so the outflow stops. The polynomial's constant term doesn't know the tank exists. And a bigger hole. The law refits one number, its constant, from the first twenty seconds, and predicts the rest within 1.5 millimetres. The polynomial refits all five of its numbers from the same twenty seconds; its forecast blew up in twelve tries out of twenty. Why such a difference? One number is easy to pin down in twenty seconds. Five numbers aren't: many combinations fit a short stretch equally well, and they disagree wildly as soon as the data run out. Two splits looked alike for both models; two others separated them completely. Only the second kind tests the claim we wanted to make. A random split tests interpolation. If you claim more, test more.

4:09

Interpolation is not discovery. Part three: interpolation is not discovery. A degree-eight polynomial in time fits our one-metre drain to 0.99 millimetres, as well as the noise allows. But it knows nothing about tanks. Ask it about a minute after the last reading, and it says eighteen millimetres; two minutes after, eighteen centimetres: a tank that refills itself. And it can't say anything about a drain from another level, because the level isn't in it. Notice what made the polynomial in time fail. Its variable is the clock, not the state of the tank. A law in the level carries over to any start, because the level is what the water responds to. Many flexible models behave like this: excellent between the data points, silent or wrong beyond them. A law earns the name by working where it wasn't fitted. A neural network trained on these readings would face the same question: what does it say about a level, a hole or a fluid it never saw? A formula that only reproduces the data it saw is a summary, not a law.

5:05

Distribution shift. Part four: distribution shift. Change the conditions the law will meet, and see what happens. A hole of 1.4 centimetres, almost twice the area: the power stayed at 0.4995, and the constant grew by 1.96, the area ratio. With the bigger hole, a full tank empties in about four minutes instead of eight. We chose these shifts by asking what a user of the law would change: the hole, and the fluid. Now a thick syrup through a long, thin outlet. The flow is slow and smooth, and physics expects a different law: a rate of fall proportional to the level itself. The same pipeline found a power of 1.0001. Why a power of one for the syrup? In slow, smooth flow through a long, thin outlet, the flow rate is proportional to the pressure that drives it, and that pressure is proportional to the level. That's not a failure. The pipeline worked, and the law changed with the fluid. Torricelli's law, forced onto the syrup, missed by thirty-eight millimetres. A law comes with a domain, and shift tests find its edges. A shift that breaks the law is still a result: it marks where the claim stops. Stress tests should include the conditions under which you expect the law to break.

6:22

Baselines, controls and ablations. Part five: baselines, negative controls and ablations. Baselines first. On the held-out low drain, Torricelli's law missed by 1.2 millimetres. A constant rate: seventy-three. An exponential decay: sixty-four. The polynomial: fifty-nine. Baselines should be strong: the best simple model a sceptic would propose, not a straw man that any law beats. A negative control is data where the answer should be nothing. We shuffled the readings in time. The log-log pipeline still reported a power, 0.07, with an error bar. The trajectory fit refused to look good: its error jumped to 229 millimetres. Why did the log-log pipeline report a number at all? It fits a line through whatever points it gets, and its error bar measures only the scatter around that line. It never asks whether the line means anything. A positive control is data with a known, different answer: the syrup. The pipeline found it, as it should. Negative controls catch pipelines that always find something. Positive controls catch pipelines that can't find anything new. And ablations remove one ingredient at a time. Drop the readings below half a metre, and the uncertainty of the power grew thirteen-fold. Swap trajectory fitting for finite differences, and the bias came back. An ablation that changes nothing is useful too. It tells you which parts of the pipeline the result doesn't depend on, and which ones it does. Every claim deserves a rival, a control that should fail, and an ablation that shows what each part contributes.

7:53

Recap. Let's recap. Run many reasonable pipelines, and report the spread. Make the split match the claim: new starting levels, new ranges, new holes. Random rows test only interpolation. Fitting what you saw isn't discovery. Shift tests find a law's domain. And baselines, negative and positive controls, and ablations keep the claim honest. And report every test, including the ones a law failed. Next video: falsification, reproducibility, and people in the loop.

Which experiment tells them apart?. We watched our tank fall from one metre to sixty centimetres, a minute and three quarters. Torricelli's law fits that stretch to 0.9 millimetres. So does a rival: a rate that's a straight line in the level. An exponential decay comes close. Which experiment tells them apart fastest? Starting a minute-long run from ninety centimetres, inside the stretch we'd seen, the laws differ by about one and a half noise widths. Starting from ten centimetres, by seventeen. We ran it: Torricelli's law missed by 1.4 millimetres, the other two by twenty-eight and twenty-five. Falsified. The best next experiment is the one where your rival laws disagree most. 1:06

Discriminating experiments. Part one. Agreeing with data can't prove a law, but disagreeing can refute one. So design experiments that could refute a law, and better still, experiments that will refute one of the rivals, whichever is true. In 1964, John Platt called this strong inference: devise alternative hypotheses, then experiments that would exclude some of them. Platt's point was speed. A field that tests one favourite hypothesis at a time moves slowly. A field that pits alternatives against each other in every experiment moves fast. The training data were one drain, watched from one metre down to sixty centimetres. We tried ten starting levels, from ten centimetres to one metre, each for one minute. For each possible starting level, we predicted a minute of draining with each law, refitted on twenty noise draws to include their uncertainty, and scored how far apart the predictions were, in units of noise. Near the levels we'd already seen, the laws agree. The disagreement grows as the start gets lower, and peaks at ten centimetres. Down there, the exponential slows the fall far more than Torricelli's law, and the straight line far less. There's a simple reason. The rival laws were fitted to the same stretch, so they're forced to agree there. Away from it, nothing holds them together, and their differences grow. Discriminating doesn't mean extreme. Ten centimetres is an ordinary level for this tank. We just hadn't watched it yet. The most informative experiment is chosen before it's run, by asking where the laws disagree.

2:34

Closing the loop. Part two: closing the loop. A closed loop repeats this automatically: fit the surviving laws, choose the next experiment where they disagree most, forecast it before seeing it, drop any law whose forecast misses by more than five noise widths, refit, and repeat. We ran that loop twelve times from fresh noise. It settled on Torricelli's law after one experiment every time. Choosing the next starting level at random instead took two experiments on average, and up to eight. Neither loop ever dropped the true law. Why does choosing well help so much? A random start often lands near the levels already seen, where the laws still agree, and that run tells you almost nothing. Of the forty random loops, twenty-three settled after one experiment and nine after two; one took eight. Choosing well saved experiments, and in a real lab, experiments are the expensive part. The loop also knew when to stop: with one law left, there was nothing left to discriminate. In a closed loop, people set the rules, and the loop applies them without getting tired or hopeful. Automation raises the bar for verification. A loop that runs experiments on its own needs the same honesty as a person: forecasts recorded before results, a stated rule for dropping a law, and a log of every decision. A closed loop is falsification at machine speed. It's only as trustworthy as its rules.

3:52

Pareto fronts with trust. Part three: Pareto fronts with trust. Usually we plot complexity against error and pick a law on the front. But error isn't the only thing we care about. Fitted on all three drains, Torricelli's law, with one constant, misses by one millimetre. The power law with a free power: also one, with two constants. The degree-four polynomial: 1.2 millimetres, with five. The straight line: 6.7. A constant rate and an exponential: thirty-seven or more. Notice the power law with a free power. It fits as well as Torricelli's law, but its second constant only says one half. On the Pareto front, the simpler law dominates it. Now add trust columns. Does the law stop at an empty tank? Torricelli's does: its rate goes to zero with the level. The straight line keeps falling below zero; the exponential never empties at all; and the polynomial's rate at an empty tank isn't zero either. These are checks you can run without a single new reading: put the level to zero in each formula, and ask what happens. And stability. Refitted on twelve noise draws, Torricelli's law put the empty time at 479 seconds, within 0.2. The polynomial said 448, with a spread of five seconds. The truth is 479. A trust column can be anything you would otherwise check by hand: units, limits, symmetries, and whether the law survived the stress tests of the last video. Here the trust columns didn't change the winner; they explained why it wins. When they do change the winner, that's worth knowing before you publish. Put physical sense and stability next to error and complexity, and the choice usually becomes obvious.

5:25

Reproducibility beyond code. Part four: reproducibility beyond code. Same readings, same code, one setting changed. Differences of neighbours on a log-log plot: 0.531. Smooth over eleven readings first: 0.502. Over forty-one: 0.501. Fit trajectories: 0.500. Sharing the code isn't enough if a default changes between versions. Record the data version, every setting, the random seeds and the software versions. And report how much the answer moves with the noise itself: across ten noise seeds, it stayed within a thousandth of one half: 0.4992 to 0.5005. A simple habit helps: save a short file next to every result, with the data version, the settings and the seeds, written by the same script that produced the number. In our tank, one changed default, from smoothing to plain differences, moved the answer more than the noise did across all ten seeds. We saw the same thing in Module five: the pipeline is part of the result, so it belongs in the report. Publish the data and the simulated checks too, not just the code, so others can rerun the calibration and the controls, not only the fit. Reproducible means another person, with your data and your record, gets your number.

6:36

People in the loop. Part five: people in the loop. Many checks in this module are judgement calls: which rivals are plausible, which controls should fail, which physical limits matter, like a rate that must vanish when the tank is empty. A person who knows tanks would reject the straight-line law on sight, whatever its fit. Build that knowledge in as explicit checks, and let people review what the search proposes, especially when an automated loop runs the experiments. Explicit checks also make the judgement visible. A rule that is written down, like a rate that must vanish at an empty tank, can be read, argued with and changed. A hunch can't. In this module, a person chose the rival laws, the syrup control, and the rule that a tank's rate must vanish when it's empty. None of those came from the data. And when a loop runs experiments on real equipment, a person should be able to stop it, see why it chose each experiment, and review every decision. The point isn't to slow the search down. It's to aim it: at plausible rivals, at controls that mean something, and at experiments worth running. Human oversight isn't a step after discovery. It belongs inside the loop.

7:41

Recap. Let's recap. Choose experiments where rival laws disagree, and forecast them before running them. A closed loop does this quickly, and needs explicit rules and records. Put trust on the Pareto front: physical sense and stability next to error and complexity. Record everything that can change a result. And keep people inside the loop. And design experiments that could prove you wrong. In the lab, you'll stress-test a tank law and choose the next experiment. Next module: extensions of symbolic regression.
