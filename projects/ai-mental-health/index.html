About the project

What this project is about

Studying how emotional context changes what large language models do, how people behave toward AI, what the structure of spontaneous thought says about repetitive thinking, and how AI predictions can support clinical decisions in mental health.

This project looks at artificial intelligence and the mind from several directions. The first asks how large language models behave when they meet emotionally charged content: with Dr. Ziv Ben-Zion and colleagues, we showed that anxiety-inducing narratives change not only what LLM agents say but what they do, steering them toward less healthy choices in a budgeted grocery-shopping task, in line with stress-linked decision biases seen in people. The second turns the question around and asks how people treat AI: in a controlled experiment with 1,684 participants, politeness toward a conversational AI declined over time and eroded faster than in human-human interactions, while human-like avatars kept people polite for longer. The third looks at the structure of thought itself: the Associator, a model trained on about 126,000 Hebrew word-association pairs, describes how free associations unfold, and nonlinear models built on these measures predicted rumination and repetitive-thinking questionnaire scores, also in an independent sample. The fourth asks how AI predictions should reach clinicians: in a pilot study with 22 physicians, letting clinicians set how a decision-support system weighs the chance of remission against side effects gave its antidepressant recommendations the highest perceived usefulness and led to the most revisions of their first choice (33.3% of cases). Together, the work aims for AI tools in mental health that are reliable, transparent and keep people in charge.

Approach and outcomes

The methods the project relies on and what it has produced so far.

Methods

  • Large language model agents
  • Emotional (anxiety) priming experiments
  • Statistical testing with multiple-comparison correction
  • AI-driven clinical decision support
  • User studies with physicians
  • Multi-criteria aggregation of AI predictions
  • Controlled online experiments on human-AI interaction
  • Word-association modeling with machine learning
  • Nonlinear prediction models validated on an independent sample

Key outcomes

  • Evidence from 2,250 runs with three LLMs that anxiety-inducing narratives shift LLM agents toward less healthy choices (npj Artificial Intelligence, 2026)
  • Evidence from 1,684 participants that politeness toward a conversational AI erodes over time, faster than between people, and that human-like avatars slow the decline (Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, 2025)
  • The Associator, a model of associative strength trained on about 126,000 Hebrew word-association pairs, whose thought-dynamics measures predicted rumination and repetitive-thinking scores in an independent sample (Cognitive Science, 2026)
  • A prototype decision-support system for antidepressant choice, tested with 22 psychiatrists and primary care physicians (Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2026)
  • Design guidance for psychiatric decision support: clinician-adjustable weighting gave the highest perceived clinical usefulness

When AI suggests an antidepressant, who sets the priorities?

AI can predict a patient's chance of remission and of side effects for each antidepressant, but how should those odds be combined? In a prototype tested by 22 physicians on realistic cases, three versions were compared: raw probabilities, fixed expert weights, and weights the physician sets with sliders. The slider version was rated most useful and changed physicians' first choice most often (33.3% of cases); fixed expert weights changed the fewest (15.8%). An exploratory pilot with simulated cases.

Based on: Bridging algorithmic prediction and clinical agency: an exploratory pilot study of AI-augmented physician antidepressant choice

Do you still say please to AI?

1,684 people each did two tasks with an AI chat assistant. Their politeness slid prompt after prompt, ending 8–10% lower by the last message, and faded faster than in a benchmark of people chatting with people. Younger participants were less polite overall, and an AI shown with a human face kept people more polite, while a robot icon made no difference. Short single-session chats in one cultural setting.

Based on: Mind Your Manners: The Dynamics of Politeness in Human-AI vs. Human-Human Interactions

Can the shape of your thoughts predict rumination?

We built a model, trained on about 126,000 Hebrew word associations, that scores how people's free associations unfold. These measures had no straight-line link to rumination questionnaires, but nonlinear models predicted the scores, and still did in 213 new people (3 of 4 tests, 46–60% of the variation explained). It is not a diagnostic test.

Based on: Capturing Thought Dynamics: Modeling the Structure of Spontaneous Associations
