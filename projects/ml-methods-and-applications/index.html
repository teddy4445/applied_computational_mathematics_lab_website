About the project

What this project is about

Developing machine-learning methods for feature selection, AutoML, optimization and anomaly detection, and applying machine learning to climate, language and social questions.

This project gathers the lab's work on machine-learning methods and on applying them to new problems. On the methods side, we develop algorithms that make models more accurate, explainable and reliable: FSPL tunes filter and embedded feature selection together as one pipeline and was tested on 90 datasets; OFEE builds feature ensembles that balance explainability and performance; a definition of feature-selection stability inspired by Lyapunov stability captures resilience to data drift; SAT-PP prunes decision trees with Boolean satisfiability while keeping their accuracy; SubStrat speeds up existing AutoML tools by searching on a small, representative subset of the data; and FAME, an optimizer based on a triple exponential moving average, was tested across 22 deep-learning architectures. We also study data that does not stay still: a genetic-algorithm ensemble that keeps learning under concept drift, a convex hull volume method for spotting out-of-distribution samples, Learning via Surprisability for anomalies along timelines, and a benchmark of 12 anomaly detection methods on temporal social-media graphs. On the applications side, machine learning lets us predict lightning-ignited wildfires worldwide and show that climate change has raised their risk, tailor fire weather indices to each country, analyse how audiences react to climate change videos, tell which large language model wrote a text, help writers correct the functional style of Russian texts, and ask whether the shape of a family tree predicts how satisfied relatives are with one another. Along the way, a more accurate algorithm for stiff matrix exponentials uses a learned decision tree to pick the right method for each matrix, and a vision-based learning model shows how collision-free collective motion can emerge in locust swarms.

Key outcomes

  • FSPL, a meta-learning approach that finds the best filter and embedded feature selection pipeline, tested on 90 datasets (International Journal of Applied Mathematics and Computer Science, 2023)
  • SubStrat, a subset-based strategy that shortens AutoML runs on large datasets (Proceedings of the VLDB Endowment, 2023)
  • FAME, a triple-exponential-moving-average optimizer evaluated on 22 deep-learning architectures (Machine Learning, 2026)
  • A convex hull volume-based anomaly detection method that performed comparably to state-of-the-art techniques on ten datasets (International Journal of Data Science and Analytics, 2025)
  • Global explainable models of lightning-ignited wildfires showing that climate change has steadily raised their risk (Scientific Reports, 2025)
  • Country-specific fire weather indices: a decision tree per country reached an ROC AUC of 0.86, against 0.69 for the best standard index (npj Natural Hazards, 2025)
  • Attribution of texts to GPT-3.5, GPT-4 or Bard with 88% accuracy from linguistic features (Mathematics, 2026)

Short explainers of the research in this project.

Pick feature selection methods together, not one at a time

Often two feature selection methods run in a row, a filter and then an embedded method, and we argue they should be chosen together. FSPL learns from past datasets which pipeline suits a new one, searching with a genetic algorithm. On 90 classification datasets with 24 possible pipelines, no single pipeline was best everywhere; FSPL picked the best one for about half of the datasets and improved accuracy by up to 5.98% over the next best meta-learning method.

Based on: FSPL: A Meta-Learning Approach for a Filter and Embedded Feature Selection Pipeline

Make AutoML 76% faster by using less data

AutoML tools search among millions of pipelines, and every try is slower on big data. SubStrat shrinks the data instead: a genetic algorithm finds a small subset that keeps the full data's entropy, the AutoML tool runs on it, and a short run on the full data fine-tunes the result. Across 18 datasets and three AutoML tools it cut computation time by 76.3% on average while keeping 95.85% of the best model's accuracy.

Based on: SubStrat: A Subset-Based Optimization Strategy for Faster AutoML

Prune a decision tree without losing accuracy

Pruning shrinks a decision tree but usually costs accuracy, which in medicine may be unacceptable. SAT-PP turns the tree's paths into Boolean satisfiability constraints and simplifies them one outcome class at a time, keeping the unpruned tree's accuracy. On breast, lung and cervical cancer datasets, accuracy and F1 score matched the unpruned trees exactly while query time fell by 6.8% on average. Three small datasets, and for random forests the speed-up wasn't significant.

Based on: Decision tree post-pruning without loss of accuracy using the SAT-PP algorithm with an empirical evaluation on clinical data

How does a locust swarm move without crashing?

In our simulation each agent sees only its surroundings and learns how to move over generations, adding to the group's shared knowledge. In a swarm of 30 locusts moving on the ground, the learned swarm moved together almost as well as a model trained only to flock (0.70 vs 0.75) but avoided collisions far better (0.74, against 0.17 for the flock-only model). Even hundreds of simple rules captured only 93% of the behaviour. A flat, two-dimensional simulation.

Based on: Collective evolution learning model for vision-based collective motion with collision avoidance

Can a model be both accurate and easy to explain?

Feature ensembles combine many feature selection runs, but existing algorithms don't treat explainability as a key factor. OFEE intersects feature sets to balance explainability and performance, with no parameters to tune. Measuring explainability by how small and how stable the feature set is, across nine canonical datasets it beat other feature selection algorithms on average by more than 8% on size and more than 7% on stability. Nine datasets, and two of many possible ways to measure explainability.

Based on: An algorithm to optimize explainability using feature ensembles

If your data shifts, will your model pick the same features?

Stable feature selection means more reliable models, clearer explanations and less exposure to attacks, but common stability definitions miss resilience to data drift and unevenly missing values. We proposed a new definition inspired by Lyapunov stability: small changes to the data should cause only small changes to the selected features. Across 90 datasets it was statistically different from the classic record-stability measure, and we describe three scenarios, each best suited to one of three measures. There's no single best measure.

Based on: A new definition for feature selection stability analysis

A more accurate matrix exponential for stiff problems

The matrix exponential solves systems of linear differential equations, and stiff matrices make it hard to compute accurately. Our algorithm builds on Putzer's method, solving its coefficient equations analytically and then approximating them. On seven kinds of stiff matrices from 3×3 to 1000×1000 it was more accurate than other algorithms at several sizes, with costs growing at about the same rate. A decision tree, built with machine learning and genetic programming, picks an efficient algorithm for each matrix, since no single algorithm wins everywhere.

Based on: More Numerically Accurate Algorithm for Stiff Matrix Exponential

Can an algorithm spot trouble brewing on social media?

A temporal graph records who interacts with whom, and when, and catching anomalies as they emerge could flag trends, threats, outbreaks or financial shifts. We put twelve data-driven anomaly detection methods to the same test on two temporal graphs, from Twitter and Facebook, looking for unusual group interactions. The pattern was unclear: no method was reliably best, which shows the field needs new approaches for networks that never stand still. Two platforms, and a benchmark of existing methods.

Based on: Temporal graphs anomaly emergence detection: benchmarking for social media interactions

What happens when the ground shifts under an AI model?

When the world changes, the patterns a model learned can drift away. Instead of one model watching for drift, we built a two-level ensemble: a population of models, each with its own drift detector, under a global model, evolved by a genetic algorithm and improved with AutoML. On synthetic datasets it significantly outperformed a single model with drift detection, especially for unknown or mixed drift, and declined only sub-linearly as drift sped up. Real-world drift may differ.

Based on: Pulling the carpet below the learner’s feet: Genetic algorithm to learn ensemble machine learning model during concept drift

Tighten the lasso: finding outliers with a convex hull

Wrap the data in a convex hull, like a rubber band around every point: outliers stretch it, so removing one shrinks the hull's volume far more than removing a typical point. Our method peels off points until the volume stops changing much. Against seven widely used anomaly detection methods on ten datasets it performed comparably to the state of the art, and a quick test tells in advance which datasets suit it. It struggles with ring-shaped data and is heavy to compute.

Based on: Tighten the lasso: a convex hull volume-based anomaly detection method

What would a computer notice if it paid attention like us?

Inspired by surprisability, our instinct to focus on the unexpected, Learning via Surprisability transforms long, high-dimensional timelines by measuring how far each moment deviates from expected behavior. On sensor data with cyber attacks it had the highest AUC (82.3%, against 54.5% for Isolation Forest); on 30 years of causes of death it showed HIV deaths peaking in 2004–2005; and it traced words across 233 State of the Union addresses. Results stay interpretable, though another method had a higher F1 on the sensor data.

Based on: Interpretable transformation and analysis of timelines through learning via surprisability

FAME: a neural network optimizer that breaks the lag

Popular optimizers smooth the noisy gradient with a moving average, which notices changes in trend late. FAME (Fast Adaptive Moment Estimation) uses a triple exponential moving average, which tracks trends with less lag. Tested on 22 architectures, from small convolutional networks to vision transformers, on classification, detection and segmentation, it gave the best results in 88.46% of the models' experiments and needed 23.7% fewer training epochs, at about 3% more memory and computation per epoch. Computer vision tasks only.

Based on: Break a Lag: Triple Exponential Moving Average for Enhanced Optimization

Can AI hear the style of a text?

A Russian language model with an attention layer, trained on 578 texts whose three expert votes all agreed, told business, academic, journalistic and literary writing apart 72% of the time. Its red highlights helped experts fix texts in the wrong style about a third faster and lifted non-experts' correct fixes from 67% to 78%. It is still wrong on about one text in four, so it points and you decide.

Based on: Computer aided functional style identifcation and correction in modern Russian texts

Can you tell which chatbot wrote this?

We gave GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and Bard the same 5,000 questions and compared the 15,000 answers on length, vocabulary, parts of speech and sentence structure. Each model had its own habits: Bard tended to write shorter answers with more personal pronouns, and GPT-3.5 used the most nouns. A simple classifier on these features named the source 88% of the time, against 33% for guessing, but tone gave nothing away: all three were positive in about 53% of answers. English only, single questions with no chat history, and the models have changed since.

Based on: Whose LLM Is It Anyway? Linguistic Comparison and LLM Attribution for GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and Bard

Can we predict which lightning strikes start a wildfire?

Lightning fires are rarer than human-caused fires but add substantial carbon emissions and account for most of the burned area in some regions. Our global models separate the two and estimate the chance that lightning ignites a fire from weather and vegetation; XGBoost reached 91.6% accuracy and held up on a year it never saw, with rain on the day mattering most. Each fire type needs its own model, and from 2014 to 2021 the global risk of lightning-sparked fires rose by about 1% a year.

Based on: Global lightning-ignited wildfires prediction and climate change projections based on explainable machine learning models

Can One Fire-Danger Formula Work for the Whole Planet?

We tested the three classic fire-danger formulas on over 7 million wildfires in 160 countries. Rules tuned to each region beat one formula for the whole planet.

Based on: Fire weather indices tailored to regional patterns outperform global models

What makes viewers side with a climate video?

We analysed 129 climate change videos on YouTube, tagging who presents each one and how it frames the problem, and an AI scored whether each comment section sided with the science. Videos presented by scientists had more than four times the odds of a supportive comment section than politicians' videos, and solution-framed videos over seven times the odds of blame-framed ones. Replies inside conversations leaned more supportive than lone comments. One English-language snapshot from 2021, and supportive comments don't show that anyone changed their behaviour.

Based on: Audience engagement with climate change content on YouTube: an analysis of video attributes and user interactions
