A B S T R A C T
Service dogs perform complex tasks in cognitively demanding environments, yet their welfare during training is 
often inferred from performance rather than measured directly. Several behavioral and physiological measures 
characterize how dogs respond to training, but researchers rarely apply them concurrently to the same animals. 
For this reason, we compared three such measures in seven purpose-bred Labrador Retrievers from a single litter 
during standardized early-stage training: facial behaviors associated with negative affective contexts (coded 
using DogFACS), urinary cortisol, and observer-based behavioral assessments by experienced trainers and non- 
expert observers. For each measure, dogs were ranked within the cohort according to that measure's directional 
convention, and all pairings were compared using Kendall's tau (τ). Rankings varied substantially across mea­
sures. Expert assessments agreed most strongly with urinary cortisol (τ = 0.67), non-expert assessments agreed 
with cortisol at a similar level (τ = 0.60), and expert and non-expert assessments agreed strongly with one 
another (τ = 0.65). By contrast, facial-behavior rankings showed no agreement with cortisol (τ = 0.00) and weak 
to very weak agreement with the observer measures (τ = 0.25 and 0.14). Because this was a small, closely related 
cohort assessed without a non-training control condition, these results cannot establish the validity of any 
measure. They instead show that measures can characterize the same dogs differently, highlighting the meth­
odological challenges of welfare assessment in working dogs and the need for larger, adequately designed 
studies.
1. Introduction
Service dogs (Canis lupus familiaris) are trained to perform complex 
tasks in demanding, unpredictable environments while maintaining 
focused attention and close coordination with their human partners 
(Cobb et al., 2021; Helton, 2009). Dogs working in support roles are 
valued for their contribution to handler well-being, and interaction with 
dogs more broadly has been associated with benefits across physical, 
social, and psychological domains (Gee et al., 2017; Handlin et al., 2011; 
Howell et al., 2022; Odendaal & Meintjes, 2003; Pendry & Vandagriff, 
2019). Although much is known about the benefits dogs provide to 
people, relatively little is known about how the dogs themselves 
perceive and experience their work (Dawkins, 2012).
This gap is compounded by a more fundamental difficulty: "stress" is 
not directly observable, as it is a physiological and psychological 
response to a perceived challenge that can only be inferred from indirect 
indicators such as endocrine activity, behavior, or expressed affect 
(Beerda et al., 1997; Morm`ede et al., 2007). These same indicators also 
reflect physiological activation, arousal, engagement, or negative af­
fective states that are not specific to stress, yet researchers often treat 
them as interchangeable. This conflation matters because any inference 
about stress depends on the biological or behavioral process being 
measured, the temporal scale over which that process operates, and 
when the measurement is collected. Each measure provides only a 
partial snapshot of an animal's response within a particular temporal 
window, rather than a complete representation of its overall state. 
Moreover, individual variation in prior experience, rearing environ­
ment, and training history further complicates interpretation, as these

Welfare-related responses in dogs are assessed using a broad range of 
methods, including ethograms quantifying discrete behaviors, cardiac 
measures such as heart rate and heart rate variability, and indicators of 
positive affective state such as exploration and play (Beerda et al., 1997; 
Cobb et al., 2021; van der Staay et al., 2025). This study focuses on three 
approaches to characterizing welfare-related responses in dogs that 
differ in both the type of information they capture and the temporal 
scale over which they operate.
The first involves identifying and quantifying facial muscle move­
ments associated with negative affective contexts, including fear, frus­
tration, and pain (Bremhorst et al., 2022; Caeiro et al., 2017; Mota-Rojas 
et al., 2021). Although these movements are frequently examined in 
welfare research, several are context-dependent and may also occur 
during affiliative, communicative, or attentional interactions, meaning 
their presence alone does not necessarily indicate a negative affective 
state (Canori et al., 2025; Capitain et al., 2025; Pedretti et al., 2022).
A second approach involves measuring cortisol, an endocrine indi­
cator of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activity (Morm`ede 
et al., 2007; Sheriff et al., 2011). Cortisol can be quantified using 
several biological matrices, including blood, saliva, and urine, which 
differ in their sampling characteristics and temporal resolution. Salivary 
cortisol is frequently used to examine relatively acute HPA-axis re­
sponses, whereas urinary cortisol reflects cortisol secretion pooled over 
the period preceding urination (Beerda et al., 1996; M¨ostl & Palme, 
2002). In the present study, we selected urinary cortisol because sam­
ples could be collected noninvasively without restraint or additional 
handling, minimizing the possibility that the sampling procedure itself 
altered physiological activation. This advantage comes with reduced 
temporal specificity, as urinary cortisol cannot be attributed to a discrete 
event occurring immediately before sample collection. Accordingly, 
urinary cortisol was used here to characterize relative physiological 
activation over the observation period, rather than as a measure of an 
acute response to the one-hour training session.
The third approach is qualitative behavioral assessment (QBA), in 
which experienced or naïve observers evaluate a dog's overall behav­
ioral expression using descriptors such as relaxed, confident, or tense 
(Paul et al., 2005; van der Staay et al., 2025; Wemelsfelder et al., 2001). 
Rather than focusing on discrete behaviors, QBA captures the expressive 
quality of behavior as perceived by human observers. These assessments 
therefore depend on observer interpretation and may vary with an in­
dividual's experience, expectations, and familiarity with canine 
behavior. Human-interpretable descriptors may also introduce anthro­
pomorphic attribution, particularly when perceived behavioral expres­
sion is interpreted as a direct representation of the animal's internal 
state. Observer-based instruments may further combine conceptually 
distinct features, including perceived affective state, attentional focus, 
engagement, and responsiveness to cues. Some of these features reflect 
trained performance as well as current behavioral state, so composite 
scores are best interpreted as broad summaries of observer perception 
rather than unitary measures of welfare.
Taken together, these approaches provide different temporal and 
functional perspectives on the same animal. Facial behavior captures 
rapid, moment-to-moment changes in behavioral expression, urinary 
cortisol integrates HPA axis activity over a preceding period, and 
qualitative behavioral assessment summarizes behavioral expression 
across a broader observation window (Cohn et al., 2007; M¨ostl & Palme, 
2002). As a result, measurements collected from the same dog during the 
same training session may characterize that individual differently 
depending on the process and temporal window being sampled. 
Agreement among these approaches therefore cannot be assumed, yet 
few studies have directly compared how measures operating across 
different biological, behavioral, and temporal scales characterize the 
same individuals.
Given these methodological differences, the goal of the present study 
was to compare three approaches used to characterize welfare-related 
responses in a cohort of dogs enrolled in early-stage service dog 
training. Specifically, we examined the extent to which urinary cortisol, 
facial behavior, and qualitative behavioral assessment yielded similar or 
different characterizations of individuals within the cohort. The 
observed sessions consisted of foundational obedience, handling, so­
cialization, and environmental exposure rather than role-specific service 
tasks. This cohort provided a relatively homogeneous population whose 
members shared age, rearing environment, husbandry, and training 
curriculum. This shared background reduced variation in several life- 
history factors that might otherwise complicate comparisons among 
assessment methods. As a descriptive pilot involving a small and closely 
related cohort, the study provides an initial comparison of relationships 
among these assessment methods within this group and is intended to 
inform the design of future studies evaluating welfare-related responses 
in broader canine populations.
2. Materials and methods
All procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the 
Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) under Protocol 
66167 through the University of Missouri College of Veterinary Medi­
cine. Human participant components were deemed exempt under 
Institutional Review Board (IRB) guidelines (Exemption Category 2), as 
no identifiable or sensitive information was collected.
2.1. Study population
Seven purpose-bred Labrador Retrievers (4 males, 3 females) 
enrolled in a formal service dog training program at Duo Dogs Inc. 
(Maryland Heights, Missouri, USA) were included in this study. All seven 
dogs were from a single litter, approximately six months old at the start 
of data collection, and shared the same early-rearing environment, 
husbandry, and training program. At the time of data collection, all dogs 
were progressing through Stage 1 of training and had not yet begun task- 
specific placement training. All seven dogs were observed during four 
weekly training sessions, yielding 28 session-level observations. All 
sessions were conducted indoors at the training facility under climate- 
controlled conditions. No elimination breaks occurred during the ses­
sions, and no urine was voided between pre- and post-session sample 
collection.
2.1.1. Training program description
Stage 1 followed a structured curriculum delivered by the same lead 
trainer throughout all four weeks. Sessions were held weekly from 5:30 
to 6:30 PM in the same designated training space at the facility, where 
all dogs performed the same set of tasks each week. The curriculum 
targeted foundational skills common to the AKC Canine Good Citizen 
framework: basic obedience (sit, down, stay, name recognition, handler 
focus), impulse control, loose-leash walking, novel-object desensitiza­
tion, handling and body-manipulation techniques, and exposure to un­
familiar handlers and staff. Each week built on the same core tasks, and 
every dog completed the same activities in each session, ensuring 
consistent exposure to tasks, handlers, and environmental stimuli across 
the cohort.
2.2. Video recordings
All training sessions were recorded using two stationary Canon 
VIXIA HF R800 cameras positioned in opposite corners of the training 
room and one handheld Panasonic HC-V785 camcorder operated within 
the room. The Canon cameras remained in fixed positions throughout 
each session and were positioned consistently across sessions. Observer- 
based assessments were conducted using the stationary recordings, and

2.2.1. Facial behavior coding
Facial behaviors were coded using ELAN with a customized anno­
tation template. First, we identified the onset, duration, and offset of all 
facial behaviors in the selected recordings. These behaviors are defined 
as one or more facial muscle movements that individuals produce and 
combine in distinct temporal bouts. Once all facial behaviors were 
identified, we coded each one according to the protocols established in 
DogFACS (Waller et al., 2013). We recorded all observed facial muscle 
movements, including Action Units (AUs) and Action Descriptors (AD), 
for each facial behavior. In our statistical analyses, we focused on in­
dividual facial muscle movements that previous literature has identified 
as potential indicators of negative affect in dogs, such as those associated 
with fear, frustration, and pain (see Table 1; Bremhorst et al., 2022; 
Caeiro et al., 2017; Mota-Rojas et al., 2021). It is important to note that 
the training regimens involved relatively low levels of physical activity 
and did not include tasks expected to cause pain. These movements 
remain context-dependent, however, as canine facial expressions can 
arise in various social, physiological, and environmental contexts. For 
example, panting (AD126) may occur in response to thermoregulation, 
physical exertion, or emotional arousal. Accordingly, we did not inter­
pret any single movement in isolation as evidence of a particular af­
fective state. This approach acknowledges the contextual flexibility of 
individual facial movements while retaining behaviors repeatedly 
associated with negative affective contexts in prior studies.
Coding proceeded on an event basis across all available handheld 
recordings. Every dog contributed coded events from multiple re­
cordings (range: 12–21 recordings per dog), and the number of coded 
facial events varied among individuals (range: 50–159). Handheld 
filming was opportunistic, and the duration for which each dog's face 
was visible and codable was neither standardized nor quantified. Event 
counts therefore represent raw frequencies rather than rates and cannot 
be interpreted independently of differences in observation opportunity. 
Per-dog recording coverage is reported in the electronic supplement.
Inter-observer reliability was assessed on a random 10% of the video 
footage prior to full coding. Two measures were used: percentage 
agreement on the presence of facial behavior events and Wexler's ratio 
for agreement on the composition of facial muscle movements within 
each event. Agreement on event presence was 100%, exceeding the 
conventional 70% criterion. The average Wexler's ratio was 0.703, 
meeting the 0.70 criterion applied in DogFACS reliability assessment. 
For within-cohort rankings, dogs exhibiting more of the candidate 
movements listed in Table 1 received rankings closer to 1, whereas those 
exhibiting fewer received rankings closer to 7.
2.2.2. Observer-based assessments
Observer-based evaluations used a brief Likert-scale survey assessing 
perceived calmness, stress, focus, responsiveness to verbal and visual 
cues, and engagement during sessions (Table 2; Wan et al., 2012). 
Evaluations were completed independently by five experts (with ≥5 
years of professional experience in canine behavioral assessment) and 
five non-experts (with no formal behavioral education) recruited 
through a dog boarding and training facility. All raters reviewed the 
same full-session stationary-camera footage for each dog, were blinded 
to study aims and handler identity, and identified dogs by collar color to 
reduce identification bias (Camerlink et al., 2022). No rater had prior 
familiarity with any of the dogs in the study. Ratings were completed for 
each dog in each of the four sessions, then averaged across sessions and 
within each rater group to produce a single composite score per dog.
The six survey items captured multiple dimensions of the dogs' 
observed behavior, including perceived affective state and task perfor­
mance. Items addressing calmness and signs of stress related most 
directly to perceived affective state, whereas focus, responsiveness to 
verbal and visual cues, and engagement reflected aspects of task per­
formance that may also be influenced by training history. Accordingly, 
the composite was treated as a summary of overall observer impression 
during the session rather than as a validated index of a single underlying 
construct. For within-cohort comparison, lower composite scores 
received rankings closer to 1, reflecting a less favorable overall observer 
impression, whereas higher composite scores received rankings closer to 
7.
2.2.3. Urinary cortisol sampling
Urine samples were collected noninvasively before and after each of 
the four training sessions using a free-catch method with a telescopic 
collection device (Hennessy et al., 1997). Handlers collected pre-session 
samples at home immediately before departing for the facility to mini­
mize potential confounding effects of car travel and arrival at the 
training environment on baseline cortisol concentrations (Rooney et al., 
2007). Handlers transferred these samples to the research team upon 
arrival, where they were held in an insulated transport cooler with cold 
packs. After each session, handlers took their dog to void, with up to 15 
min allowed for collection. All samples were aliquoted and stored at 
−80 ◦C following session completion. Samples were subsequently 
shipped to the Saint Louis Zoo Endocrinology Laboratory for analysis.
Cortisol was measured using a commercially available enzyme 
immunoassay (DetectX Cortisol EIA, K003-H; Arbor Assays, Ann Arbor, 
MI, USA). Prior to assay, 5 µL of β-glucuronidase/arylsulfatase (Roche 
Diagnostics 10–127–698001) was added to 500 µL of each urine sample 
and incubated at 37 ◦C for 24 h. Hydrolyzed samples were diluted 1:100 
with assay buffer and assayed according to kit instructions. Creatinine 
was measured using a commercially available enzyme immunoassay 
(DetectX Urinary Creatinine EIA, K002-H; Arbor Assays), with samples 
diluted 1:20 in deionized water. Cortisol concentrations were normal­
ized to creatinine concentration (ng/mg Cr) to account for urine dilution 
(Weber et al., 2010). All samples were assayed in duplicate in a single 
assay for each analyte; mean intra-assay coefficients of variation were 
8.9% for cortisol and 7.8% for creatinine. Because we ran only one assay 
per analyte, we did not calculate inter-assay variation. Assay validation

Sample timing was considered when determining how urinary 
cortisol values were summarized. Maximum urinary cortisol concen­
tration has been reported approximately three hours after a stressor in 
dogs (Rooney et al., 2007), whereas post-session samples in the present 
study were collected within 15 min of the session ending. These samples 
therefore cannot be attributed specifically to the preceding training 
session and likely reflect cortisol secretion accumulated over a broader 
preceding period (M¨ostl & Palme, 2002). Using post-session values alone 
would consequently imply a degree of temporal specificity that urinary 
cortisol does not provide. Across the 20 available pre- and post-session 
comparisons, cortisol increased in 11 and decreased in nine, with no 
consistent direction across dogs or sessions. We therefore summarized 
each dog with a single mean concentration across all available samples, 
treating this as an index of relative physiological activation across the 
observation period rather than as a response to any individual session. 
Session-level values for all dogs are reported in the electronic supple­
ment, and dogs with higher mean cortisol received rankings closer to 1.
2.3. Data analysis
Each measure produced a single within-cohort ranking for each dog. 
To ensure comparability, all measures were oriented using the same 
convention: a ranking of 1 was assigned to the dog showing the highest 
level of that measure's putative welfare-relevant response (i.e., the most 
negative-affect facial behaviors, the highest mean cortisol, or the lowest 
observer composite score). This shared orientation means that positive τ 
values indicate that two measures ordered the cohort in the same di­
rection. We did not assume that this ordering reflects the true severity of 
stress; it is a descriptive device for comparing measures. Rankings are 
relative to the cohort by construction: a dog ranked 1 on a given measure 
scored highest within this group of seven, which carries no implication 
about absolute level.
Rankings derived from facial behavior, expert observer assessments, 
non-expert observer assessments, and urinary cortisol were compared 
pairwise using Kendall's tau (τ), a nonparametric measure of rank 
agreement. Ties were assigned the same rank. We report τ magnitudes

3. Results
Counts of facial behaviors associated with negative affective contexts 
ranged from 25 to 146 across dogs (median = 60). Expert composite 
scores ranged from 16.00 to 28.00 (median = 23.00), and non-expert 
composite scores ranged from 21.75 to 26.25 (median = 24.25). Mean 
urinary cortisol ranged from 65.11 to 137.59 ng/mg Cr across the five 
dogs with complete data, and individual creatinine-corrected samples 
ranged from 39.77 to 308.69 ng/mg Cr. Within-cohort rankings for all 
four measures are summarized in Table 3; full dog-level descriptive 
values are provided in the electronic supplement.
Pairwise rank agreement ranged from no agreement to strong 
agreement (Fig. 1). Agreement was strongest between expert observer 
assessments and cortisol rankings (τ = 0.67) and between non-expert 
assessments and cortisol rankings (τ = 0.60), with strong agreement 
between expert and non-expert assessments (τ = 0.65). By contrast, 
facial-behavior rankings showed no agreement with cortisol (τ = 0.00), 
weak agreement with expert assessments (τ = 0.25), and very weak 
agreement with non-expert assessments (τ = 0.14).
4. Discussion
The present descriptive pilot compared how three approaches to 
assessing welfare-related responses characterized the same cohort of 
dogs during standardized early-stage training. Across the cohort, urinary 
cortisol, facial behaviors, and observer-based assessments did not 
consistently characterize individual dogs. Observer-based assessments 
showed strong agreement with one another and with averaged urinary 
cortisol values, whereas facial behavior rankings differed substantially 
from both. Collectively, these findings indicate that assessment methods 
may yield different characterizations of the same individuals, even when 
observations are collected from the same dogs during the same training 
period. This is a methodological observation about how these measures 
relate to one another; it does not indicate that training affected welfare 
in any particular direction, which the present design was not built to 
assess.
One possible explanation for the differing patterns observed across 
measures relates to the structure of Stage 1 training. Dogs were required 
to sustain engagement and task performance for approximately one hour 
of training while encountering multiple exercises and environmental 
challenges. Under these conditions, overall behavioral presentation may 
have remained relatively stable, yielding similar observer-based as­
sessments, whereas facial expressions fluctuated in response to indi­
vidual moments within the session and urinary cortisol reflected 
physiological activation pooled across a broader preceding window. 
Consequently, the differing patterns observed across measures may 
reflect the distinct aspects of the training experience captured by each 
method rather than conflicting assessments of the dogs themselves.
A comparable divergence between behavioral and physiological 
measures has been reported in dogs. Rooney et al. (2007) followed 31 
male Labrador Retrievers of similar age and rearing history through 
introduction to kennels and found no significant correlations between 
any behavioral variable and urinary cortisol/creatinine ratio across the 
population, despite the sample's homogeneity. They concluded that 
urinary cortisol/creatinine ratio responded to the imposed stressor more 
predictably than behavioral measures did, while cautioning against 
exclusive reliance on any single welfare indicator. These findings align 
with the broader observation that behavioral and physiological mea­
sures need not covary, though this descriptive pilot provides no basis for 
privileging one measure over another. With five dogs contributing 
complete cortisol data and seven contributing behavioral and 
observer-based data, these results instead illustrate the methodological 
consequences of treating different welfare-related measures as 
interchangeable.

The differing patterns observed across assessment methods highlight 
an important challenge in evaluating welfare in working dogs. No single 
measure captures every aspect of an animal's response during training. 
Physiological measures, facial behavior, and observer-based assess­
ments each provide distinct perspectives on canine responses and are 
influenced by their own assumptions and methodological constraints. 
Consequently, agreement among measures should not necessarily be 
expected in all circumstances, nor should disagreement be viewed as 
evidence that one approach is inherently superior to another. Instead, 
these findings emphasize the importance of interpreting each assess­
ment within the context of what it measures and the temporal window 
over which that information is collected.
4.1. Limitations
The present findings should also be interpreted in light of several 
important methodological limitations. First, pre-session samples 
collected at home provided a baseline measure prior to training, but the 
study lacked a non-training control condition. Therefore, training- 
related changes could not be distinguished from physiological varia­
tion that may occur across the same period on a non-training day. Sec­
ond, urinary cortisol values were averaged across pre- and post-training 
samples, limiting interpretation of acute within-session physiological 
responses. Third, all dogs originated from the same litter, shared early 
developmental experiences, and completed the same training program. 
This homogeneity was advantageous for a methodological comparison, 
since it limits the influence of breed, age, and rearing differences on how 
the measures ordered individuals. However, it also reduces the inde­
pendence of observations and limits generalizability beyond this cohort. 
The urinary cortisol dataset was further reduced by incomplete sample 
collection, and facial behavior frequencies were not normalized for 
visible face time or observation opportunity. Another consideration is 
that the observer composite combined items addressing perceived af­
fective state with items addressing task performance, so its agreement 
with cortisol is harder to interpret than agreement between two mea­
sures of a single construct. Finally, observations were limited to four 
weekly training sessions during Stage 1 of training and therefore 
represent only a brief developmental period. Because the measures only 
rank dogs relative to one another, the highest- and lowest-ranked dogs 
will always emerge, even if none experienced meaningful stress. With 
cohorts of five to seven, these rankings are also sensitive to individual 
dog placement. Therefore, the rankings should not be read as absolute 
levels. Collectively, these considerations emphasize that the present 
findings should be interpreted as descriptive observations.
Despite these limitations, the present study offers a preliminary 
methodological comparison of three assessment approaches used in 
canine welfare research. By concurrently evaluating urinary cortisol, 
facial behavior, and observer-based assessments in the same dogs, this 
study highlights the challenges of comparing measures that differ in 
both temporal resolution and the type of information they capture. 
These findings provide a foundation for future studies designed to 
evaluate how welfare assessment methods relate under more controlled 
experimental conditions.
Future work should incorporate baseline and non-training control 
sampling, analyze physiological responses at the session level rather 
than using aggregated measures, include larger cohorts of unrelated 
dogs across multiple training programs, and provide more detailed de­
scriptions of training activities and environmental exposures. Coding 
facial behavior outside the training context would establish baseline 
rates of individual facial movements for each dog, allowing training- 
session behavior to be evaluated relative to that individual's typical 
expression. Standardizing or quantifying codable face time would 
further allow facial-event frequencies to be expressed as rates, 
improving comparisons among individuals with different observation 
opportunities. Mixed-effects models accounting for handler, session, and 
observation opportunity would also make better use of session-level data 
than the aggregated approach used here. Additional physiological 
measures, such as heart rate variability, could help determine whether
