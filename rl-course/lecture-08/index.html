Lecture 8Deep reinforcement learning

Neural networks as value functions: automatic differentiation, deep Q-learning in detail, why online deep RL is hard, and the tricks that make deep learning and RL work together, such as target networks, experience replay, double DQN, prioritised replay and dueling networks.

Episode 27 · 5:25

From a Q-table to DQN

Tic-tac-toe in about ten lines of Python. Then we swap the table for a network.

Swap the table for a network, keep the idea.

TranscriptEpisode 27

Tic-tac-toe

Let's start with code. In class, we built Q-learning for tic tac toe. Nine squares, so nine actions. Plus one for a win, minus one for a loss, zero for a draw. First question, always: is your reward really what you want? Stop and check. It's usually not trivial. γ is point nine, so Bit looks many moves ahead. And ε is point one. The reward only arrives at the end of the game. Every other move gets zero.

The weights are the table

The Q-table starts at zeros. And here's a nice way to see it: the model's weights are the table's values. Look for the policy in the code. There's no policy function anywhere. The policy is implicit. It's the argmax of Q. The update itself is four lines. Elegant to the bone. Short, readable, and it really works. If both players share one table, they play symmetrically. With two tables, they can play differently. And against a random opponent, you learn the best play against a random player. Nothing more.

Our run

We ran it: Bit plays crosses against a random opponent, for forty thousand games. After a hundred games, playing greedily, he wins about sixty percent. After forty thousand: about ninety six percent, and he almost never loses. By then, the table holds about two thousand board positions. A nice trick from class: make a video of the table filling in. It looks like abstract art. These are the values of the first move. The centre comes out on top. Another trick: plot the table's average value over time. It's jagged, and more jagged with a bigger ε. Ninety six percent sounds great. But remember who the opponent is. Against a strong opponent, it would have a lot more to learn.

Least squares TD

Now, gradient descent uses each experience once, and then throws it away. That's not very sample efficient. Batch methods ask a different question: which weights best fit everything we've seen so far? For linear TD, there's a closed form. Least squares TD: build a matrix and a vector from the data, then solve. Plain TD costs about n operations per step, for n features. Least squares TD costs about n squared. Better answers, more work. It's the same answer TD converges to. You just get it directly, from the data you already have. And you can update it incrementally, one transition at a time, without solving from scratch. Least squares TD extends to q values too, and even to whole policy iteration.

Experience replay

Or keep it simple: experience replay. Store the transitions in a buffer. Then sample old ones at random, and update on them again. Each experience gets used many times. And random sampling breaks up the order. One catch: old data came from old policies. Replay is off-policy, whether you like it or not. Replay is also a form of batch learning: go over the past again and again, a little at a time. One more benefit: rare, important experiences don't get lost. They stay in the buffer.

A network instead

Now swap the table for a neural network. The observation goes in. One q value per action comes out. Q-learning's update becomes a loss to minimise: the squared TD error. But we stop the gradient through the target. We treat the target as a constant. So it's not a real loss. It just happens to have the right gradient. And we only score the action we took. The other outputs get no feedback on this step. With three actions, the network has three outputs. Each output is one action's q value. The rest is plain deep learning: a mini batch, a gradient step, and an optimiser like

DQN

Adam. Put it all together, and you get DQN, from DeepMind, more than ten years ago. A network that maps the screen to action values. ε-greedy exploration. A replay buffer. A second, frozen copy of the network for the targets, refreshed every ten thousand steps or so. And an optimiser. Function approximation, bootstrapping, off-policy replay. All three corners of the deadly triad. And it works. Every piece we met in this module, finally in one place. DQN learned to play dozens of Atari games from the raw pixels, with one recipe. How it gets away with that is the story of the next module.

Cloud servers

One more example from class, closer to real work: a cloud of servers. There are small servers and large servers. Jobs come in small, medium and large. The actions: send a job to a small server, send it to a large one, or wait. The reward mixes completion time, utilisation and idle machines. I like giving each part a weight, and playing with the weights. Is that reward correlated with what the client wants? Stop and check. Again. With ε fixed, the reward curve stays noisy. Lower ε slowly, and it settles.

Module seven, done

And that's module seven, done. Too many states, so Bit generalises: with features, with tiles, and now with networks. He knows the deadly triad, and the one table to remember. And the code went from a table to a network, without changing the idea. Next module: deep reinforcement learning. Where everything is a vector.

Minus two

I didn't remember this lecture's number from the start. But from the end, it was minus two. That's the more important one. Module eight: deep reinforcement learning. A quick recap. All those lovely promises, the global optimum and all. You got so excited. They live over a table. Visit every state and action infinitely many times. Fun in theory. In practice, you won't. Linear functions: decent near the data, bad far from it. Neural networks: great in practice, and in theory we know nothing. Today: why neural networks, how they learn, and what goes in and out.

Good features?

The question hanging over this whole course: is our feature vector any good? Good means that states with similar rewards get similar features. But if we knew which states had similar rewards, we wouldn't need to approximate in the first place. So instead of designing the features, let the model learn them. That's what deep learning offers: features learned from data, tuned for your reward.

Deep is not neural

First: deep learning is not the same as neural networks. Deep learning means chaining a learning model into itself, again and again. Deep decision trees exist too. Chain trees, and you just get a bigger tree. Not my best example. Draw linear regression as circles and arrows, and you get a perceptron. Stack linear layers, and you still get something linear. You have to break the linearity somewhere. That's the activation function. Don't let anyone tell you stories. Linear is cool, and we add a little non-linear.

Universal approximation

And then something remarkable. A big enough network, layer, activation, layer, can approximate any continuous function. We tried it: a bumpy curve, and one hidden layer of tanh units. Two units: a rough sketch. Five units: close. Twenty units: you can barely tell them apart. In theory, any continuous function. In practice, big enough can mean very big. Our own fits, each trained for thirty thousand steps. The error drops about four thousand times, from two units to twenty.

Two advantages

As an engineer, I'll tell you: neural networks aren't cheap. They're expensive, hard to train, and hungry for data and memory. Most of the neurons sit idle most of the time. You keep many, and use few. Deep learning has exactly two advantages. Don't let anyone tell you otherwise. One: expressiveness. Two: it learns its own embedding, its own representation. In reinforcement learning, that second one is gold. You don't have to invent a clever state representation.

Chess, three ways

Take chess. How do you write down a position? Option one: the board, eight by eight, with a code for each square. Wasteful: most squares are empty. Option two: a list of the pieces, each with its type and its square. Option three: the starting position, plus the list of moves so far. That's what chess notation does. Cheapest, for short games. Which is best? The one that predicts rewards best. And you don't know that in advance. And the best one can change from one task to the next. A deep network says: I don't care about your representation. I'll find a good one for you.

Everything is a vector

Which brings me to my favourite claim: everything is a vector. A matrix? Fold it into a vector. A tensor? Fold it twice. A dictionary? Sort it, and line it up. The proof: every data structure sits in memory. And memory is a vector. Even an image: it's just a long row of pixel values. Physics says so. Complaints, please, to the physicists. Not to me. So whatever your state looks like, you can feed it to a network.

Autodiff

To train a network, we need gradients. Writing them by hand would be miserable. So we draw the computation as a graph. Inputs flow forward, one operation at a time, to the output. A tiny value network: s times w one, through tanh, times w two, plus a bias. With s equal to two, tanh of one is point seven six, and the value comes out at three point two nine. Now sweep backwards, and multiply the local gradients along the path: three, times point four two, times two. Two point five two. That's the gradient for w one, in a single backward sweep. Where paths merge, you add. That's automatic differentiation. Libraries like JAX do it for you: NumPy, plus gradients, plus a compiler for fast hardware. In code, you write the forward pass. The library writes the backward pass for you.

What goes in and out

So what goes in, and what comes out? A value network is just regression: a vector in, one number out. Any regression network works. Put AlexNet in, for laughs. It won't work. Or maybe it will. A policy network: a state in, an action out. Or a state and an action in, and q out. Or a state in, and one output per action. A network with three outputs? Those are three actions' q values. Why not feed in the whole history? It gets huge fast. And the M in MDP stands for Markov: the present state is enough. Our maths works because we don't need the history. The right choice depends on your problem, not on fashion. Next time: why training these networks online is so hard.

Not i.i.d.

Supervised deep learning has a quiet assumption: the samples are independent and identically distributed. Shuffle the data, take a mini batch, take a step. Each gradient is an honest sample of the whole picture. Online reinforcement learning breaks that. We update on every new step. And each step grows out of the previous one. The data is strongly correlated. Each new sample looks a lot like the last one. The gradient keeps pointing the same way, for a while. Like learning about a whole city from one street.

Order matters

Here's what that does. We fit a straight line to a wavy curve, with online gradient descent. First, the data arrives like a trajectory: left to right, then back again. Our own run: two hundred points, four passes, step size point zero five. The line chases wherever the data is right now, and forgets the rest. Its error swings up and down, and never settles. Now the same points, shuffled. The line settles right next to the best possible fit. Shuffled, the error ends within a few percent of the best a line can do. In order, it swings between about one and three times that. Same data, same model. The only difference is the order. Now imagine that with a network of millions of weights, on a whole game.

The fixes

So how do we fix the order? Experience replay: store, shuffle, and sample mini batches. Replay does double duty: it breaks the correlation, and it reuses each sample many times. Dyna-style planning does something similar, with samples from a learned model. Better optimisers help too, like momentum. So do better online algorithms, like eligibility traces. And then there's the vanilla trick: eight cores, eight simulations in parallel. Eight independent streams. Solved by engineering, not by maths. Totally legit. Eight streams also means eight times the data, for every second you wait.

The triad, again

But look at what DQN combines. A neural network. Bootstrapped targets. And a replay buffer full of old policies. Function approximation, bootstrapping, off-policy. The deadly triad, all three. Is this a sane thing to do? Researchers measured it, in twenty eighteen. Unbounded divergence turned out to be rare. More common: the values blow up early, then come back down. They called it soft divergence. Soft divergence still hurts. While the values are wrong, the policy built on them is wrong too. So yes, the guarantees die. But in practice, the patient usually survives.

Good-enough optima

So why does it work at all? It converges to a local optimum that's good enough. The downsides: it's very sensitive to where you start. Which optimum you land in depends a lot on luck, and on where you start. And it nails the problem it trained on, but doesn't generalise. The original DQN trained a separate network for every Atari game. Feed it lots of data from one narrow region, and it becomes an expert on exactly that region. Later work trained one model across many games.

A good start

About that starting point. A random network is usually bad, because most of the space is bad. Now imagine that a good starting point fell from the sky. Then you search aggressively around it. That's the idea behind imitation learning: start from a human's good policy, and improve from there. In many real projects that's exactly the plan: a human demonstrates first, and the agent takes over. Start well, and half the battle is won. And even then, the training curves take millions of episodes, and stay noisy.

Target networks

Back to the triad. TD chases its own tail: change the weights, and the target moves too. The fix is a target network. A frozen copy of the weights, used only to compute the targets. Train the main network for a few thousand steps against a target that stands still. Then copy the weights over. Think of a dog chasing its own tail. Now tie the tail to a post for a while. You can't backpropagate from one network into the other. So you copy the weights, every so often. That's the trick. It breaks the feedback loop at the heart of the triad. Copy too often, and the target moves again. Copy too rarely, and you learn from stale values. A frozen target turns each stretch of training back into ordinary regression.

Double DQN

Remember double Q-learning? Q-learning was too optimistic, so we split it in two. DQN already has two networks. So the online network picks the best next action, and the frozen one scores it. That's double DQN. Same idea, almost for free. The online network is fresh, so it picks well. The frozen one is independent enough to score honestly. And in that twenty eighteen study, the double versions blew up far less often. They looked at the largest values each agent predicted. Plain Q-learning blew up most often.

Recap

Correlated data: shuffle it, or run in parallel. Moving targets: freeze them. Optimism: split the job in two. None of these is a proof. Together, they made deep Q-learning work in practice. Next time: not all samples are born equal. Plus a few tricks that make deep networks fit reinforcement learning better.

Dogs, cats and wolves

Not all samples are born equal. DQN samples its replay buffer uniformly. Every memory has the same chance. In class, I used a classifier for dogs, cats and wolves. Tons of dog and cat photos. Very few wolves. Your model is already good at dogs and cats. On wolves, it's badly wrong. So where should you spend your energy? On the wolves. Prioritised replay does exactly that. A sample's priority is the size of its last TD error. In practice, you sample by priority, and keep updating each priority as the errors change. A simple idea. Nothing fancy. And it doesn't have to be wolves. Any rare, surprising transition gets more attention.

The numbers

Some numbers. A buffer with forty five dogs, forty five cats and ten wolves. The errors: point one on dogs, point two on cats, and two on wolves. Uniform sampling picks a wolf ten percent of the time. By priority: about sixty percent. So the wolves get sampled six times more often. The model spends its time where it's wrong. Ten wolves out of a hundred, and they get most of the attention. The catch: now the samples don't match what the agent really sees. Even more off-policy. Importance weights fix part of that. Samples that got picked a lot count a little less.

Multi-step targets

Next: multi-step targets. Take n real rewards, then bootstrap, with the double Q trick. The meaning: follow your behaviour for n steps, then act greedily. Still a policy improvement. Just a less greedy one. It trades bias against variance, and leans less on bootstrapping. On-policy for n steps, then greedy. That's a well-defined target, and still a good one. Less bootstrapping, fewer explosions. Researchers saw it: bigger n, safer values. In that same study, three steps blew up much less often than one, and ten steps less than three.

Inductive bias

Now the other direction: make the network fit reinforcement learning. Copying architectures from supervised learning is a start. But we shouldn't stop there. The question for reinforcement learning: which inductive biases help an agent that acts? Deep learning's big wins came from the right inductive bias. Convolutions for images, because a cat looks like a cat anywhere in the picture. Gating, as in LSTMs, for long memory. So which layers for your agent? Convolution only if the input has spatial structure, like Atari pixels. A feature vector has none. Sequences? Recurrent networks. Fully connected layers are fine for finding relations among features. And attention, lately, does great everywhere.

Dueling networks

One architecture was made just for reinforcement learning: the dueling network. Split q into two parts: how good is this state, v, and how much better is each action, the advantage. Then add them back up, and you still get q. The state value gets an update from every action. So it learns faster. On a straight, empty road, every action is about the same. The state value carries it. Learn the state once, and the differences separately. Less to learn for every action. And the advantages tell you which action is best, which is all the policy needs. A small change to the network, and often a big change to learning.

Capacity

Capacity. In supervised learning, more data plus a bigger network usually means better results. In reinforcement learning, bigger networks typically do better too. But they're also more prone to the deadly triad. So scale up, but watch your values for explosions. More capacity isn't free. It's one more thing to

The wall

tune. And generalisation can bite. Picture a maze with a wall. On one side of the wall, you're one step from the goal. On the other side, it's a long way around. Nearby states, very different values. A network smooths over the wall, and the values leak through it. This is called leakage propagation. Errors leak through the wall, and then they spread. Monte Carlo sees how episodes really end, so it learns there's no way through. But it still underestimates near the wall. TD keeps telling itself: I'm almost there. And its error grows, step by step. Monte Carlo leaks too. But it doesn't feed on its own leaks. And that's not a bug in one algorithm. It's what generalisation does near a sharp edge.

Learning about many things

Behind the triad and the leakage is the same problem: the wrong generalisation. Better representations help. One way to get them: learn about many things at once. Predict the values of other policies. Predict the next observations. Predict signals besides the reward. Every extra prediction shapes a better shared representation. Almost for free. Even if you only care about one reward, learning about many things helps. That's where module eleven picks up, later in the series.

Module eight, done

And that's module eight, done. Three modules ago, Bit couldn't act. Now he acts, generalises, and trains deep networks without blowing up. Mostly. Correlated data, moving targets, optimism and leakage. Each one has a fix, and none of the fixes is a guarantee. And it all combines with the tricks from earlier: traces, double learning, and replay. Next module: planning. Bit learns a model of the world, and starts to imagine. See you there.
