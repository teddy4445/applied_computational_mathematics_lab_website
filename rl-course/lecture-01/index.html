Introduction to Reinforcement LearningPart 1 · Foundations

Lecture 1Introduction

What reinforcement learning is and why it matters, the agent–environment loop, rewards, returns and values, what an agent can know about its world, and the three parts an agent can have: a policy, a value function and a model. Meet Bit, the little robot who learns by trying.

By the end you should be able to say what an RL agent is trying to do, write down the return and the value, and name the parts an agent can have.

Episode 1 · 3:14

Bit learns to get the star

How do you learn when nobody hands you the answers? Meet Bit, a tiny robot who learns the way kids do: by trying.

Learn on the way. Your choices shape your data.

TranscriptEpisode 1

Recorded forever

Quick warning: everything here is recorded forever. So think before you speak. Okay. This is Bit, and this is reinforcement learning. Let's start with the simplest part.

What you already know

You know machine learning, right? Supervised: you have X, you have Y, and you look for a function from X to Y. Unsupervised: you only have X, and you look for structure. Clusters, anomalies. What do they have in common? The data was there in advance. And the learning was passive, like a scientist watching nature. With me so far?

How does a kid learn?

Now forget computers. How does a kid learn? Show him a thousand pictures of cats and dogs, and he still doesn't know what's good or bad. He needs feedback. Here's a boiling pot. Bit touches it. Ouch! Pulls back. And never does that again. Did Bit have data in advance? No. Bit started with nothing.

Why learn on the way?

Why learn along the way, and not at the end? If Bit only learned at the end, it would keep touching the pot, over and over, until the statistics kick in. By then, evolutionarily, Bit is toast. So we learn on the way, from rewards and punishments. That's reinforcement learning.

Two differences

Two things make it different. One: the data arrives along the way. Bit acts, the world answers with what Bit sees and a reward, Bit scribbles it down, and tries again. Two, and this is the important one: the learning is active. What Bit learns changes what data Bit gets next. Bit avoids the pot, so it never samples the pot again. Bit just shaped its own future data. Cool?

Isn't that just a loss function?

Isn't that just a loss function? Great question. How many forests do you burn before a supervised model learns that burning forests is bad? Better to catch the signal in the middle, not at the end.

Chase the total

And Bit doesn't chase the next reward. It chases the total. Grab every shiny pebble, and the gate shuts behind you. Skip them, and you reach the big star.

Learn, decide, reach goals

There are many definitions of intelligence. One I like: the ability to learn, make decisions, and reach goals. Supervised and unsupervised learning don't make decisions. Bit does.

A bit of history

A bit of history. The industrial revolution automated our muscles. The digital revolution automated memory and math. My vote for the software that changed humanity the most? Excel. Now we're automating decisions. Every AI wave promises us the Terminator. Let's see if we get there this time.

The big question

So, two things to remember. In reinforcement learning, the data arrives along the way, and your choices shape your data. With me so far? Next time: is every goal really just… stars?

Is every goal just stars?

Last time, Bit got the big star, and I left you with a question. Is every goal really just… stars? Today we answer it. Kind of.

The reward hypothesis

If you take one slide from this whole course, take this one. The reward hypothesis: any goal can be written as maximising one number, the total reward. And notice the word I'm stressing: hypothesis. It's an assumption. No proof, no guarantee. I'll say it thirty times, and then thirty more. Without it, we don't know how to work. So we take it, right or wrong. And notice: the reward is a scalar. One number per step, not a list.

Rewards in the wild

Does it feel right? Let's check. Chess: plus one for a win, minus one for a loss. Flying a helicopter: time in the air. Managing a portfolio: more money, less risk. A walking robot: distance? Speed? Not falling over? Maybe all three. Each goal is one circle, but the number behind it can hide a lot of choices. And if your goal really can't be written as a sum of rewards? Then RL won't work well. Find something better, write a good paper, and put me on it.

Writing the reward is an art

In RL I'll show you lots of science: formulas, proofs, the works. One thing isn't science. It's art: writing the reward. Watch. I reward Bit for every star he touches. So Bit finds one star and taps it. Forever. Technically, perfect. Okay, I reward distance walked instead. Bit walks in circles. Very far. Going nowhere. So: plus ten for reaching the star, and minus one for every step. Quick lesson in rewards: want something to happen, make it positive. Want to avoid it, make it negative. Now the fastest route wins. Bit was never wrong. My reward was. Writing the reward is where most of the funny bugs in RL come from. You'll see plenty this season.

Reward vs. loss

If you know supervised learning: reward is your loss, flipped. There you minimise, here you maximise. And reward isn't a property of the world. A robot vacuum leaves less dust. Is less dust physically good? No. That's us, interpreting. The reward is a number we put on top of the world. Observations and actions belong to the world. The reward is our interpretation, sitting outside it.

The return

Now, what Bit actually chases: the sum of all the rewards from now on. We call it the return, G. G for goal. Easy to remember. Notice it starts from now. Philosophically: don't cry over spilt milk. What you did so far, you can't change. The goal is from here on. And as Bit moves on, the return moves with him. It's always measured from wherever he stands.

Value: the return you expect

Quick question: standing here, does Bit know his return? No! The future hasn't happened yet. So we use the next best thing: the value of a spot, the return Bit expects from there. Why expects? Because the world can be random. Chess has no dice. Backgammon does. Same move, different outcomes. Look at this board: all your pieces, against one sad king in the middle. High value. You can feel it. For the other side? Very low. And look: the value talks about states. The actions are hiding inside the future rewards.

Now or later?

Now it gets interesting. Bit's fuel is running low. Straight ahead: three easy stars. To the left: a fuel station, and no stars at all. The greedy move grabs the stars, and runs dry two steps later. The detour costs stars now, and wins more later. That's value: not what you get now, but everything you can expect from here.

Value of an action: q

One more. A dungeon game. Bit stands on a tile: gold ahead, and lava on both sides. What's this tile worth? Good? Bad? Honestly, weird. So we ask a better question: how good is this tile, together with the move I'm about to make? This tile plus gold: very high. This tile plus lava: very low. That's the action value, q. q for quality. Value asks: how good is this place? q asks: how good is this place, if I do this?

Look around the corner: Bellman

Last trick. The return is the next reward, plus the return after it. So value is: reward now, plus the value of where you land. Instead of predicting the whole game, look only around the corner. Much friendlier. One step at a time: the reward you're about to get, plus a guess for everything after. We'll be back to this. It has a name: the Bellman equation. With me so far? Next time: what does Bit actually see?

Does the robot see the room?

Quick quiz. A robot vacuum has a camera. Does it see the whole room? No. It sees a slice. The room is the world. The slice is what the robot gets. That gap is this whole episode. And it's not just robots. Right now, you see a slice of this room, not the whole building.

State vs. observation

The environment state is everything true about the world: every star, every rock, every gust of wind. Bit doesn't get that. Bit gets an observation. Usually much smaller, and often missing exactly the part you need. Even Bit has a state of his own. A vacuum cleans the room, and its battery drops. The action changed the world, and the agent.

History

So what does Bit have? His history. The first observation, the action he took, the reward he got. Then the next observation, and so on. Everything, up to right now. Problem: it never stops growing. Long day, very long ribbon. History is the raw material. Everything Bit knows, he has to build from it.

Fully observable worlds

For most of this course, we'll make a simplifying assumption: what you observe is the state. In chess, that's true. You look at the board, and the board is the whole game. In symbols: the state S t equals the observation O t. We call that fully observable. With real robots and real sensors? Almost never. We'll keep that assumption for most of the course, and drop it later on.

Markov

Now, a word you'll hear a lot: Markov. By the way, Markov is a surname. A Markov process says one simple thing: I remember nothing. What happens next depends only on where I am now, not on how I got here. Formally: the chance of the next state and reward, given the state, is the same as given the whole history. Is the weather Markov? Sunny today says something about tomorrow. But not everything. Seasons! Without the history, you'll be wrong at some point. So not everything is Markov. But it's usually a not-bad approximation. And the test is simple: if adding more history doesn't help, the state is Markov. Then you can throw the history away. Good news for your memory, and your computer's. Careful: Markov doesn't mean the state holds everything. It means more history wouldn't help.

When Markov breaks

Let's see it break. Bit is in a maze, and he only sees the three by three squares around him. Here, he sees this. And here, on the other side of the maze, exactly the same thing. But here, the exit is down. And here, it's up. If Bit decides on the observation alone, he must make the same move in both. Mathematically, no choice. So he'll be wrong in one of them. Every time. And a smarter policy doesn't fix it. The information simply isn't in the observation.

The fix: remember

The fix: Bit remembers where he came from. He keeps a small state card, and updates it every step: old state, last action, reward and new observation give the new state. Now the two spots look different, and the symmetry breaks. And here's the magic: the card from one step ago already carried what mattered from thirty steps ago. Remember I said recursion comes back? It comes back a lot. That update function, u, is something we design, or something Bit learns.

Why not just observations?

Why bother? Why not just work with observations? Because if all our math lives in how the robot sees the world, we get very good at the robot's view, not at the world. We're turning a subjective truth into an objective one. Sometimes that's not easy.

Partially observable

When Bit can't see the full state, we call the problem partially observable. A poker player only sees the public cards. A robot's camera isn't told where the robot is. The world can still be Markov. Bit just doesn't know it yet. He discovers it along the way. Later in the course we'll come back to this properly. For now, the assumption stays.

Two things to remember

So, two things to remember. Bit doesn't act on the world as it is. He acts on his state: what he built from what he saw. And if that state is good, the past can go. Cool? And every other gadget lives on that state. Policy, value, model: all of them. Next time, we open Bit up and see what's inside.

Opening the hatch

Today we open Bit up. Don't worry, it's a cute hatch. Inside an agent there are up to three gadgets. A policy, a value function, and a model.

The policy

First, the policy: a map from state to action. Given where I am, what do I do? We write it pi. Don't ask me why. P was already taken, for probabilities. A deterministic policy is a compass needle: one state, one move. A stochastic policy is a spinner: one state, a probability for each move, and you sample. In code, a stochastic policy is just a table of probabilities: for each state, one per action. Then you sample.

The value function

Second, the value function: how good is this state? But careful: good for whom? Give the same chess position to a beginner and to a grandmaster. The value isn't the same. Value always depends on the policy that keeps playing from there. Same board, same pieces. Different player, different future.

The discount, γ

And a new dial: the discount, gamma, between zero and one. Every step further into the future, a reward is multiplied by gamma once more. It shrinks exponentially. Gamma equals one: the plain sum from last time. Gamma equals zero: only the next reward counts. That's a greedy algorithm! So RL literally extends greedy algorithms. Nice, no? Care only about now? Low gamma. Need to plan far ahead? High gamma, like 0.99. Gamma sets the effective horizon: roughly how many steps ahead Bit really cares about.

Bellman, with γ

With gamma, last time's recursion becomes: value is reward now, plus gamma times the value of where you land. That's the Bellman equation. Fun fact: it doesn't come from RL at all. It comes from optimisation theory. We stole it. And for the best possible value, the same function sits on both sides. If a value keeps changing every time you visit it, you're not at the optimum yet.

The model

Third gadget: the model. Until now we had sensible names. Here we gave up: the prediction model is just, the model. It predicts what the world does next: the next state, and the next reward. In chess, you move the knight and you know the next board. Easy, for you. A computer holding the board as a list of numbers needs someone to compute it. That's the model. In backgammon, the dice make the next board a distribution, so the model predicts probabilities, not one board. And rewards can be nasty: the square root of the distance, times the chance you're spotted, minus the weather outside. Good luck. One important thing: a model doesn't act. Bit still has to think with it. That's called planning. And every model is a guess. A good guess saves a lot of real-world mistakes.

All three on one maze

Let's watch all three on one maze. Minus one for every step, so the shortest path wins. The policy: an arrow in every cell. Quiz: this cell, does Bit ever reach it? No. So does its arrow matter? No! So even this toy maze has a huge number of optimal policies: any arrow, in every cell Bit never visits. The value: the number of steps to the goal, as a negative number. Standing on minus eight, where do you go? Minus seven, or minus nine? Minus seven! Always step to the highest value you can reach. That simple rule gives you the policy. Policy and value are two views of one problem: one in actions, one in states. From the values you can rebuild the arrows, and from the arrows, the values. Two perspectives, same problem. And the model: Bit's own map of the walls, with a minus one stamped on each cell. These numbers only make sense because the reward is minus one per step. Change the reward, and every number changes.

Kinds of agents

So different agents just switch on different gadgets. Value-based: keep the values, and read the policy off them. It doesn't get a policy. It derives one. Policy-based: learn the policy directly. Actor-critic: both. Spoiler: it usually works best. And separately: model-free, learning only from experience, or model-based, building a model and using it. We'll meet every one of these this season, some of them in deep-learning form.

Three gadgets

So, three gadgets. The policy picks. The value judges. The model imagines. Find them, and Bit can act. Approximate them well, and he can act well, even in huge worlds. With me so far? Next time: the four problems the whole season solves.

Zooming out

Last episode of module one! Let's zoom out. Every RL problem hides two very different problems. Two problems, and two ways to solve them. That makes four squares.

Prediction vs. control

Prediction: given a way of acting, how good is the future? You evaluate. Control: find the best way of acting. You optimise. They're tied together. The best policy has the best values, and the best values give you the best policy. And a question to think about: if we could predict everything, would we need anything else? And prediction is built in: every definition we wrote looks at future rewards. Two problems, one goal. Who remembers which? Maximise the total reward.

A classic gridworld

Here's a classic gridworld. Bump into a wall: minus one. Step on A: plus ten, and you jump to A prime. Step on B: plus five, and you land on B prime. Prediction first: if Bit wanders at random, what's each square worth? Near the walls, negative. Around A, high, even though A throws you away. Look at A itself: worth eight point eight, less than its ten. You jump to A prime, right next to a wall. B is worth five point three, more than its five: B prime lands you in the open. Now control: what's the best Bit can possibly do? Every square gets better, and the arrows head for A. Ten beats five, even with the long walk back. Notice: the best values beat the random ones everywhere. That's what good acting buys you.

Learning vs. planning

The second split: learning versus planning. A robot vacuum enters a new room. How can it plan a route if it doesn't know the room? So first, it drives around and discovers the room. That's learning: interacting with an unknown world. Then it goes home, sits, and plans the best route in its head. That's planning: thinking with a model, no new interaction. Ants do it too: wander until one finds food, then the whole colony forms a line. Learn, plan, learn, plan. In the math, learning and planning sit together. In practice, you have to explore the space, and plan over it.

The two-by-two map

Put it together and you get a two by two map: predict or control, by learn or plan. Predict while learning: how good is my way of acting, from experience. Control while planning: the best way, from a model. And the two in between. The rest of the season fills in these four squares.

Four functions

And to fill them, we need just four functions. Policy: state to action. Value: state to a number. Model: the next state and reward. And state update: from observations to a state. Find these four, and you've solved the problem. The whole course asks one question: what kinds of solutions for them are good? Each of the four is just a function. Tables, formulas, neural networks: all fair game. Deep learning, for example, can fill one of them, or several. An Atari agent might use a network just to turn pixels into numbers. Rules unknown, no answer key. Just joystick moves, pixels, and a score. Learning directly from play.

When to pick RL

So when should you pick RL at all? Say you trained a sales model for a shop. It works great for two months. Then something very sad happens: a competitor opens two shops away. The world changed under the model's feet. That's concept drift, and it's more common than you'd think. RL keeps learning while it runs, so it can keep up. Supervised learning splits fit and predict. RL doesn't have to. It learns while it acts. A world that changes, and decisions in a sequence. That's where RL shines. And sometimes it's spectacular: robots that learn to walk, run and jump, with nobody writing down the steps.

Module one, done

Module one, done. That's most of the definitions; from here, fewer new words and more algorithms. Next stop: a beach full of slot machines. Explore, or exploit? One state, many choices: the cleanest place to watch exploration happen.
